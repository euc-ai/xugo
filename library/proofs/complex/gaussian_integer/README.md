# gaussian_integer — the Gaussian integers inside ℂ, their norm, their division algorithm, and the primes dividing m² + 1

The Gaussian integers are the complex numbers a + bi with a and b
integers: an integral domain with a Euclidean norm, hence a principal
ideal domain, which settles which primes are sums of two squares.

## The ring ℤ[i]

A complex number is a Gaussian integer exactly when its two parts are
integers. The parts of a sum, a negation and a product are sums,
negations and products of the operands' parts, so ℤ[i] is a subring of ℂ;
as a subring of an integral domain it is an integral domain.

## The norm and the division algorithm

The norm read in ℝ is the square sum of the parts. It is multiplicative
because the square sums are, and it is zero only at zero. Dividing in ℂ
and rounding both parts of the quotient to the nearest integers leaves a
remainder whose square sum is at most half the divisor's, so the norm is a
Euclidean measure and ℤ[i] is a principal ideal domain.

| `GaussianDivision` | Dividing a by a nonzero b in ℤ[i] leaves a remainder of smaller norm than b |
| `Euclidean` | ℤ[i] is a Euclidean domain under its norm |

## Primes dividing m² + 1

The ideal of ℤ[i] that a prime p and m + i span is principal, generated by
some α, and N(α) divides N(p) = p². N(α) = 1 would make p divide m − i, and
N(α) = p² would make it divide m + i, which no natural number above 1
does; so N(α) = p, and the parts of α give p as a sum of two squares.
