XuGo is designed from the ground up for humans and agents exploring mathematics together. The central idea is that every proof is also an opportunity to improve the engine and uncover reusable mathematical structure. In this way, the prover and the theorem library evolve together.

This practice revolves around two mutually reinforcing feedback loops:

  • Difficulties encountered during proofs lead to improvements in the engine and agent instructions, making subsequent agents more effective even when starting from a fresh context.
  • Derivations that recur across chapters are factored out into shared lemmas and structures, providing cleaner abstractions for future proofs.

Fast compilation and replay allow both feedback loops to run frequently. As the library grows, its development continually improves both the tools used to build it and the mathematical abstractions that organize it.

The library

In XuGo, every proof in the corpus is a compilable, runnable Go program. Rather than a static collection of finished results, the library is a continually evolving software artifact. Every completed theorem contributes an argument that can be read, examined and reorganized. Its local constructions and the hypotheses actually used in its proof can inform later work.

A theorem's identity is its statement, not its name or file location. The organizational layer of names, packages and files is decoupled from the logical layer. Reorganizing the library is therefore a mechanical rewrite of the Go AST, with no change to its logical content. This allows the library to develop new results while continually refining its foundations.

The card graph

Work on a new mathematical chapter begins by laying out its statements before writing their proofs. Once the human and the agent agree on the chapter's scope, definitions and the order of its members, each unproved theorem is first represented as a card. In XuGo, a card is neither a TODO comment nor an informal placeholder; it is a first-class Go variable with a complete formal statement, a generic type signature and an explicit dependency list. The kernel makes the card available to the rest of the library, and the registry tracks everything that depends on it.

Because compilation and replay operate directly on this card graph, even unproved cards form a type-checked, dependency-gated DAG. Later chapters can cite earlier cards and proceed without waiting for their proofs to be completed. Flawed definitions or mismatched interfaces can be identified at compile time or during independent review. Humans can audit and reshape the mathematical architecture at the card stage just as they would with proved theorems.

Once the card graph is established, work on individual proofs can proceed independently. Cards can be assigned to separate agents and worked on in parallel, in any order. Cards provide stable mathematical interfaces for division of labor: an agent starting from a fresh context works with an explicit statement and dependencies, producing a theorem that preserves the card's exact statement and identity. Theorems that cite the card inherit its proof automatically, without requiring any changes to their own code.

The library also helps agents plan proofs. XuGo's registry indexes each theorem's statement, the definitions it uses, its actual dependencies and the open cards it relies on. When faced with a goal, an agent first queries the library to identify its dependencies, determine which results have been proved and which remain open, and find existing theorems that may help justify individual proof steps. A card's notes can also record the intended approach. The same information makes completeness auditable: every result reports the open cards it still depends on. When that set is empty, all the unproved assumptions on which the result relied have been discharged.

A proof session

Proof exploration takes place within a kernel-level prove session. Starting from a card's goal, the session builds a branching search tree. The agent chooses a proof step, generating subgoals, and passes leaves that can be closed directly to built-in closers. It can explore alternative steps, backtrack and reuse intermediate nodes discovered on other branches.

Searching a proof-state tree or embedding automated provers in an interactive loop is nothing new. What sets XuGo apart are four design choices:

  1. Interaction between the agent and the engine is governed by the kernel's inference rules. Every probe reports whether it succeeded or failed, with a concrete reason. Refutation derivations involving search are recorded. During exploration, the agent can try different paths, backtrack and reorganize its work. Once a derivation has been recorded, other agents can read it, cite its result and verify it independently. Proof results persist across agents, contexts and interruptions: even when an agent is replaced, its context is exhausted or a session is interrupted, previously established results retain their identity and remain verifiable. Multiple proof nodes can be developed and checked independently, then assembled according to their explicit dependencies. This makes it easy for multiple agents to collaborate across mathematical domains.
  2. Automation is organized around the intermediate propositions and premises that the author states explicitly. Only the facts named in a proof step are passed to a closer; all other hypotheses are cleared for that call. The proof text therefore shows which facts support each conclusion. The agent can focus on decomposing the mathematical argument: choosing which intermediate conclusions to introduce, which lemmas to apply and which conditions to establish first. These choices remain explicit in the proof, making its structure visible and checkable. When a step fails, the agent can focus on the local proposition and identify the problem, such as a missing membership condition, a structure that has yet to be established or a lemma that is too weak.
  3. Once every leaf of the exploration tree has been closed, the emitter follows the recorded derivation and exports the intermediate propositions, branches and references to Go in dependency order. The agent can inspect exactly what each node proves without reconstructing the argument from the conversation or the history of tactic calls. Even before the main proof is complete, established auxiliary results can be added to the chapter for use in subsequent proofs. After export, the agent gives facts and branches meaningful mathematical names, follows the chapter's notation and citation conventions, organizes the steps into a coherent argument and identifies local results that can be extracted as shared lemmas. The agent can explore an alternative argument in another proof session or implement it directly by refactoring the Go program, a form of work well suited to coding agents.
  4. After the proof has been refined, an independent reviewer checks its readability from a fresh perspective. Can a mathematician unfamiliar with the theorem follow the argument from the proof script alone? Would the essential reasoning remain clear if all comments were removed? The reviewer examines the role of every mathematical node and looks for repeated arguments, implicit dependencies on context and unused conditions. The final script thus preserves the mathematical content verified during the session while presenting it as a coherent proof within the chapter, ready to be read, cited and further refactored.

The first loop: proving improves the tools

In this loop, each sub-agent begins work on its assigned card in a fresh context, reading the instruction files anew. Experience is not retained in individual agents' memory; instead, it is captured in the engine's code and the agents' instruction files. When an agent encounters a difficulty during a proof session, the human and the agent discuss how to address it through a new engine feature, a more precise diagnostic or a new abstraction tool.

One example is ByElementarity. The proofs of four theorems over real closed and algebraically closed fields—Ax–Grothendieck, the descent of systems of polynomial equations, the existence of lexicographically minimal points, and polynomial Brouwer—originally required manually constructing their first-order formulas, establishing closedness and supplying evaluation bridges. Together, these proofs contained forty such intermediate propositions. An agent abstracted this work into a single step. It reflects the statement into a first-order formula and constructs the model-theoretic transfer as a kernel derivation using a chain of lemmas referenced by name: the quantifier bridges of first_order, the encoding bridges of polynomial_encoding, and the evaluation lemmas of each formula chapter. Once the tactic was implemented, those intermediate propositions were removed, along with any members that were no longer referenced. The original theorem statements remained unchanged, and all four theorems remained proved. This is the strength of LCF: a tactic's power comes from the theorems already proved in the library, and its soundness is guaranteed automatically by the kernel. Every new bridge lemma added to the library extends the engine's capabilities.

This feedback loop depends on one strict rule: engine defects must be fixed at their source rather than worked around in individual proof files. Large language models are particularly good at finding workarounds. If agents were allowed to compensate for limitations in the tools with laborious local proof steps, they would consistently take the path of least resistance. Proof files would become increasingly complicated without any corresponding improvement to the engine. Prohibiting such workarounds ensures that difficulties encountered in individual proofs lead to improvements in the shared infrastructure.

The second loop: unifying mathematical and program abstraction

In XuGo, identifying abstractions in code is also a way to discover mathematical abstractions. The software-engineering principle Don't repeat yourself (DRY) corresponds directly to the extraction of shared theorems and structures in mathematics. Agents may struggle to invent deep mathematical abstractions from scratch, but they excel at recognizing recurring patterns in code, extracting common elements and refactoring programs.

A typical Lean tactic script consists of instructions that change the proof state implicitly; the goal after rw or simp is not written in the source. Authors can name intermediate facts with have, but they are not required to do so. Automation has access to the entire context, so the script does not explicitly show which premises a step uses. Two mathematically isomorphic derivations can look completely different in the source code, making it difficult for an agent to recognize the mathematical structure they share. XuGo requires proofs to make their line of reasoning explicit, and its closers see only the premises explicitly listed. As a result, recurring mathematical arguments are much more likely to appear as recurring patterns in the code, where they can be identified and refactored through the process described below. Lint enforces the mechanical requirements, while independent review assesses what automated checks cannot: whether a reader unfamiliar with the proof can follow its reasoning.

Because proofs are native Go programs, the full Go ecosystem is available for day-to-day work. Agents scan across chapters and mathematical domains for recurring patterns, concepts and parallel arguments. Examples include proofs in different chapters that independently establish the well-definedness of a quotient map on coset representatives, or proofs concerning number fields and algebraic curves that use isomorphic sequences of substitutions to compute a dimension. When an agent identifies such a pattern, it proposes a mathematical concept for discussion with the human. Does the pattern point to a previously unnamed algebraic or geometric structure? Which chapter should contain its formal definition or theorem?

The benefits of such refactoring can be assessed in four ways:

  • Search footprint: every theorem records the dependencies actually used in its proof. Has their number decreased?
  • Proof simplification: have recurring blocks of a dozen or so local Have steps across different domains been replaced by a single cite in each proof?
  • Readability: once low-level details involving cosets or coordinates are encapsulated in a named concept, are higher-level proofs easier to read?
  • Structural reuse: does the registry show more reuse across mathematical domains, and have transitive dependency sets become smaller?

Making this process routine depends on one practical prerequisite: refactoring speed. When an agent extracts a shared lemma into a foundational chapter and updates a dozen callers, compilation and replay must provide immediate feedback. In mathlib, modifying a low-level algebra file triggers recompilation of everything that depends on it, with a single check taking anywhere from tens of minutes to hours. Low-level refactoring therefore involves lengthy CI runs and manual review, making it difficult for agents to perform routinely. In XuGo, Go compiles in seconds, and replaying a chapter together with its direct dependents takes anywhere from a few seconds to a minute or two. Even after a change to the lowest-level foundations, the entire library can be replayed in roughly ten minutes on a multi-core CPU. Extracting a lemma, updating ten proofs and replaying them becomes a routine operation.

That speed comes from Go's native compilation, a lightweight LCF kernel with no typeclass search or elaboration, and modular verification. Replay stops at the statements of other registered theorems, so shared foundations do not need to be re-expanded at every citation.

The cycle compact shared chapters → fast replay → frequent refactoring → more compact shared chapters means that each new landmark creates opportunities to refine the foundational chapters it depends on. Lemmas initially developed for a particular problem are moved into more foundational chapters, and the proofs that need them are updated to cite the shared statements. Each chapter can then focus on its own mathematics. For example, the finiteness of the class group of a number field and Riemann–Roch on an elliptic curve rely on the same foundational results about invertible fractional ideals, modules with dimension, fields and polynomials. Integer lattices, fractional ideals and field extensions viewed as vector spaces also cite the same module theorems. Shared arguments are proved once and reused across an increasing number of domains, while each landmark retains only its distinctive objects and core arguments.

Deduplication is generalization

Not every recurring pattern in code corresponds to a new mathematical concept. It may simply correspond to an existing constant that has been overlooked, and abstractions based purely on code layout may conflict with mathematical intuition.

Agents therefore propose abstractions, replay verifies their mathematical soundness, and independent review and human judgment assess their mathematical significance and elegance. The constitution and review-driven deduplication reinforce each other: agents reduce each theorem to the hypotheses it actually needs, the kernel verifies that those hypotheses suffice, and review identifies recurring arguments and the chapters in which they belong. Together, these practices sustain the process of refinement.

Take ideal factorization. The code separates the properties bundled together in the textbook definition of a Dedekind domain, identifying exactly which ones are needed at each step. For a subring of a field, the conditions that every nonzero ideal has finite index and that the subring is integrally closed in the field together already imply that nonzero maximal ideals are invertible. The core factorization theorem IdealFactorizationExists requires even less: only that strictly ascending chains of ideals above the given nonzero ideal have bounded length and that maximal ideals containing it are invertible. Factoring out a maximal ideal strictly enlarges the remaining ideal, so the bound on chain length guarantees termination. Uniqueness requires only the invertibility of the maximal ideals appearing in the factorization, which is enough to cancel common factors.

Riemann–Roch illustrates the same approach. In the library's model of curves, serre_duality.RiemannRoch requires only curve data and an invertible lattice pair; the curve may be singular. Smoothness is not included as a blanket assumption. It is introduced only where it plays a specific role in recovering the classical formulation. Refactoring reveals that the heart of the theorem governs a broader class of objects, with the classical statement emerging as a specialization.

Similarly, while proving the transitivity of algebraicity, an agent needed the fact that finitely many algebraic elements generate a finite extension. It discovered that the same argument was already present in a local proof in galois_correspondence.SeparableRootFieldGalois. Extracting the argument as algebraic_element.FiniteAlgebraicAdjoin provided a shared lemma for the new proof and allowed two inline occurrences in the original proof to be replaced by direct citations. Both the new proof and the existing chapter benefited from the refactoring. A new problem revealed the independent value of an existing argument, which became a named lemma in the appropriate chapter. Every caller now shares a single verified proof. Existing proofs thus provide a growing source of reusable but as yet unnamed mathematical results.

XuGo's dependency graph is completely explicit, and the kernel knows exactly which predicates each theorem uses. This makes it possible to continually refine theorem statements so that they depend only on the hypotheses their proofs actually require, rather than the assumptions conventionally grouped together in prose. Where arguments are reused across mathematical domains, this process systematically reveals previously unnamed generalizations and potential mathematical structures. Textbook definitions group properties together at a level of granularity suited to human working memory. XuGo's explicit dependency graph allows us to distinguish those properties and identify the precise logical relationships that make them useful—and that is where new mathematics can begin to emerge.
