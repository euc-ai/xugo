Carriers Are All You Need

XuGo's claim is that simple type theory, with structures defined on carrier sets, can support a whole body of mathematics, from mathematical induction to the finiteness of the class group of a number field, Joyal's theorem on quasi-categories and the Weil conjectures for curves over finite fields. Much of the theory underlying these results is shared. The question is not simply whether a scheme can be represented using carrier sets, but whether the same principle, applied throughout the library, allows several domains to develop together. Can algebra, geometry, topology, homotopy theory and other domains form a unified body of mathematics in a single logic, verified by one kernel, rather than separate libraries with nothing in common but their kernel? XuGo argues that they can.

Simple type theory was chosen for the kernel because it is small enough to audit and because its statements read like ordinary mathematical statements. The carrier-set principle is what makes that choice sufficient for modern mathematics: conditions that would otherwise be encoded in types, instances or coercions become propositions checked by the kernel. The principle gives the library its expressive reach: no construction needs a type of its own, and the transport problem of dependent type theory never arises.

The additional work this design requires is explicit and mechanical. In return, a result proved in one chapter is available to every other chapter that needs it. This essay describes the design and makes the case for that claim.

The prejudice

The received view is that the objects of modern abstract mathematics need to be represented as types: a subfield, a quotient and a space of sections each have a type of their own. Structure is attached through instances, moving between objects requires coercions, and the dependencies between objects so common in schemes, derived functors and homotopy theory become dependencies between types. This is the design mathlib chose, and its success has made dependent types look like the price of admission, because that particular design relies on them. Familiar difficulties with this approach—motives that fail to type-check, equalities that require transport—are often regarded as inherent costs of formalizing mathematics rather than consequences of a particular design. Since simple type theory cannot adopt that approach, it is assumed to be inadequate.

The carrier-set principle offers another way: an element lives in an ordinary type, a carrier is a set in that type, and a structure is a predicate or a record over its explicit components. The principle is not new. It is how the ring library of HOL Light and the locales of Isabelle express algebraic structures, and how Bordg, Paulson and Li formalized Grothendieck's schemes without dependent types in Isabelle/HOL. The credit for that step is theirs. XuGo takes this approach further. Using carrier sets, it supports a range of modern mathematics within simple type theory: simplicial resolutions, derived functors of a non-additive functor, classification by cohomology, dimension computations by duality, and point counts derived from zeta functions, among other results.

Elements in types, structures on sets

A type is where points live and nothing more. A group is a record G containing an operation, an identity, an inverse and a carrier set; group_on(G) asserts that the record defines a group. A module is specified by a ring record and a module record, with module_on(R, M); a field is a bundle field_on(A) whose carrier is a subset of A. Each law of a structure is stated on its carrier, with membership as a guard: x ∈ c ⇒ …. A structure on the whole type is the special case in which the carrier is the universal set 𝕌. The whole-type version of a law is a view generated from the carrier-based proof, not a separate proof.

One consequence shapes the entire design of XuGo: no construction needs a type of its own. A quotient ring is the set of cosets of an ideal. A subfield is a set, and the automorphisms fixing it form a group on a set of permutations of the type. A simplicial set is a family of sets with face and degeneracy functions; a quasi-category is a simplicial set with a filler for every inner horn. A curve is a field on a set, its function field, together with two subrings representing its charts. A line bundle on the curve is a pair of lattices in a common ambient module. Each of these constructions is a value that can be quantified over in a theorem, and general theorems about its structure apply directly, without coercions, instances or transport between types.

Reading a proof as a paper

A formal proof is auditable by construction: every step is a kernel certificate, every cite command identifies a member, and every member lists the cards on which it depends. Readability, however, is not guaranteed by construction. In practice, there is often a trade-off: a proof the kernel accepts may be one that few people can comfortably read. In mathlib, proofs are conventionally written as tactic scripts. To see what a particular step establishes, the reader inspects the elaborator's state by replaying the file. The text records the moves rather than the claims. XuGo addresses this by capturing concepts as reusable abstractions. For every noun a paper would use there is a definition; for every arrow a paper would draw there is a named map; for every "clearly" there is a law. A proof of Riemann–Roch can then cite the formula for the Euler characteristic of a pair (its degree plus one minus the genus), the finiteness of the cohomology of an invertible pair, and Serre duality. It can be read at the same level of abstraction as a mathematical paper because the underlying results about additivity of the Euler characteristic, cosets, lattices and coefficient extraction have already been established as members of earlier chapters, each with its own formal statement for the reader to inspect. The abstraction is not in the comments; it is in the tree, and the proof text names it.

Organizing concepts this way also allows XuGo to grow without fragmenting. When a proof has to work out the details of cosets, the chapter on cosets is missing a lemma. When two chapters repeat the same pattern of reasoning, that pattern should become a reusable member in one of them. This is precisely what a referee looks for: mechanical issues are resolved, while decisions requiring judgment are referred back to the person. The balance between auditability and readability is therefore not struck proof by proof. It is struck once per concept, when the concept is given a name, and every subsequent proof that uses it benefits. A reader can thus follow a proof in deformation theory through obstructions, torsors and automorphisms at the same level of abstraction as in a mathematical paper, and verify each step by inspecting the member it cites.

Reuse

A landmark's dependency cone consists of the landmark itself and every member on which it directly or indirectly depends. Reuse means that cones overlap. The material unique to a landmark is largely confined to the chapter that states it and a few chapters written specifically for it, such as integer lattices and determinants for the class group, or the zeta function of a curve. The rest is shared with other landmarks.

Define a topic's cone as the union of the dependency cones of its landmarks on the landmarks page. As of November 2026, 1,941 members are used by at least ten of the 35 topics. Entire intermediate theories support several landmarks: the cotangent complex serves both directions of deformation theory, and lattice pairs are used in Riemann–Roch, deformation theory and the construction of the zeta function. The most frequently cited members are the closure laws of groups, modules, rings and fields, each stated once and used in every domain that involves those structures.

This sharing follows from the design, for reasons developed in the rest of this essay. A theorem about a structure on a carrier can be invoked with a cite command. The chapter that establishes a law need not anticipate its eventual applications, and a proof that needs the law can cite it rather than restate it. A lemma extracted from a proof belongs in the chapter devoted to its subject, regardless of which proof first required it. Proving a new landmark therefore mostly extends chapters already used by other proofs, reducing the cost of proving subsequent landmarks.

Five examples show how the same principle enables reuse across domains.

From a field extension to vanishing homology. A simple algebraic extension K(a) of a subfield is a set. Evaluation at a identifies it with the polynomial ring over K modulo the minimal polynomial of a, a hypersurface quotient K[x]/(μ). When a is separable, the minimal polynomial is a non-zero-divisor whose derivative is a unit modulo the polynomial itself. The quotient of a ring by one non-zero-divisor is resolved by the Koszul algebra. Its cotangent complex has homology only in degree one, on the conormal class of the element. The tower K, K[x], K[x]/(μ) gives rise to a transitivity long exact sequence. Its connecting map sends the conormal class of μ to its differential, acting by multiplication by the derivative, which is a unit. Thus every André–Quillen homology module of K(a) over K vanishes: the extension is étale. The same computation, with f·x − 1 in place of μ, shows that localization at the powers of an element is étale over the original ring. The argument draws on field theory, commutative algebra and simplicial homotopy as needed, without reconstructing any of the objects involved.

From a cohomology class to a ring, and from a ring to its obstruction. The cotangent cochain complex with coefficients in a module N is the dual of a based complex, so its cohomology Dn(S/R, N) inherits the general properties of such duals. In degree one, its cocycles are the conormal maps of the standard presentation R[S] → S, and its coboundaries are restrictions of derivations. A conormal map twists the split extension R[S] ⋉ N into a quotient ring that is a square-zero extension of S by N. A derivation gives a shear map from one such quotient to another. Conversely, lifting the presentation to a square-zero extension recovers a conormal map. Thus a class in D1(S/R, N) is realized by an explicit ring on an explicit set of cosets. Every extension is isomorphic to the realization of its class, and two extensions are isomorphic precisely when their classes agree, regardless of the types on which they are defined. Deformation theory then studies these same objects in a tower of rings. A square-zero thickening of a base ring is itself an extension and determines an extension class. An algebra over the quotient admits a deformation over the thickening exactly when a lift exists, which in turn is equivalent to the splitting of the pullback of the thickening. Pullback is the explicitly defined right-hand map in the cohomology transitivity sequence. The obstruction is therefore obtained by applying the connecting map to the class of the thickening, giving a value in D2. If a deformation exists, the deformations form a torsor under D1, and the automorphisms of a given deformation are described by D0. None of this requires a new construction: it is the classification of extensions applied along a sequence whose maps have already been defined in the general duality framework.

One curve, three problems. A single model of an elliptic curve suffices for all three problems: a Koszul quotient of the free algebra on two variables in the cotangent host. Its function field is the fraction field of that ring, and its two charts are the images of two such quotients in the field. The same differential machinery—the partial derivatives and Jacobians of the free algebra—establishes the smoothness of each chart, since the partial derivatives generate the unit ideal, and gives its module of Kähler differentials, free on one generator. One cohomology framework, the lattice pairs with their two-term Čech complex, gives three answers. Applied to the structure sheaf, it gives the genus, which is one; together with Serre duality and the degree of a twist by a point, it yields Riemann–Roch. Applied to line bundles of degree zero, it yields the group from which the group law on the curve's points is induced. Applied to the derivations of the two charts, it gives the space of first-order deformations, whose dimension equals the genus. The connecting map in the cotangent theory shows that the obstructions vanish because the charts are smooth. Moreover, the dualizing pair of the curve is its module of differentials, so the canonical class in Riemann–Roch theory and the tangent sheaf in deformation theory emerge from the same computation. Applying the same framework to the projective line, with the trivial pair, shows that it is rigid. Nothing was rebuilt for the second or third problem. Each abstraction introduced for the first reduces the work needed for the next.

One chapter of ideals, two geometries. In a ring in which every nonzero ideal has finite quotient, the nonzero ideals factor uniquely into prime ideals, and the invertible fractional ideals form a free abelian group on those primes. The proof requires only this finiteness condition and the assumption that the ring is integrally closed in its field. The ring of integers of a number field satisfies the finiteness condition because it is a lattice: every nonzero ideal contains a positive integer, and the quotient of the integers by that integer is finite. An ideal class is then a coset of the principal fractional ideals. Using an integer coordinate box, one finds an element of small norm in every nonzero ideal. This yields an integral representative of bounded norm for the inverse class. Since there are only finitely many ideals of bounded norm, the class group is finite. The coordinate ring of a chart of a curve over a finite field satisfies the same finiteness condition because its quotients by nonzero ideals are finite over the constants. A closed point is then a maximal ideal with a residue degree, an effective divisor is a finite product of maximal ideals with multiplicities, and the nonzero sections of a lattice pair modulo constants correspond to the effective divisors of a class. Counting these divisors by degree, with the large-degree contribution determined by Riemann–Roch, yields the zeta function of the curve, together with its rationality and functional equation. The analogy between number fields and function fields is a chapter in every textbook; here it is one chapter cited from two domains.

Two theories built on simplicial sets. A simplicial set is a family of sets with face and degeneracy maps. Its standard simplices, horns and fixed-prefix cones are defined once. In homotopy theory, this framework gives Kan complexes and their homotopy groups, the long exact sequence of a fibration, the nerve of a category, quasi-categories with their homotopy categories and mapping complexes between vertices, and Joyal's theorem: a quasi-category is a Kan complex precisely when every edge is invertible in its homotopy category. In homological algebra, the same chapters provide the simplicial rings and modules used for the cotangent complex: the standard resolution as a bar construction, the Moore complex, the Alexander–Whitney map and the diagonal of a bisimplicial set. The transitivity sequence of a tower of rings thus uses the same simplicial machinery as Joyal's theorem.

Paying for each mechanism once

The cost of the work is amortized across the library because each construction is stated once and reused in several domains. The cost is incurred once per mechanism, rather than once for every theorem that uses it. Where a textbook writes "well defined on cosets" again and again, XuGo proves it once for the induced quotient map. The table lists this and other mechanisms, showing what is established once and where it is reused. The way these mechanisms are formulated also explains why the chain from commutative algebra to deformation theory does not explode in complexity. Quotients share a common construction and an induced map; an isomorphism is established by a theorem, with a separate law for transferring each relevant property; functoriality requires an identity law and a composition law; exactness is an equality of carriers; and compatibility is expressed by a commuting square on carriers. Isomorphic carriers

A reader familiar with the transport problem of dependent type theory might expect it to reappear here in a different form. Quotient rings, polynomial rings and localizations each live on a different type from the original ring, while the cotangent construction and the algebraic closure each require a common host type. Four design decisions prevent the transport problem from arising.

Nothing is moved in order to be used. A quotient ring is a ring on a set of cosets, at its own type. A single theorem, proved directly for the quotient's operations, establishes that it is a commutative ring. From then on, every theorem about rings on sets applies to it through a cite command, just as a theorem about ℝ applies to ℝ. The same holds for localizations, polynomial rings, residue fields, free algebras, automorphism groups and rings of integers. Where a dependent type theory must identify two types and introduce a cast, here the two carriers are simply sets. No statement requires them to be the same set, because each statement quantifies over the carrier it concerns. The problem that transport solves never arises.

The host names elements; it does not embed rings. The host type used in the cotangent construction accommodates the free algebras, since these are the objects that must be constructed repeatedly within it. A ring S remains on its own type with its own operations. Only a name for each element of S is introduced into the host, chosen once through an injection; the ring structure of S is used directly at S. Three rings on three types can therefore have their cotangent complexes in a common host without moving the rings themselves. The host type for the algebraic closure follows a similar design: a copy of the base field is embedded into the host through an injection, and each extension is then used directly as a subfield.

A global object is a pair of subsets of a common ambient space. Gluing is expressed by intersection and sum. No transition isomorphism is needed, because the overlap of the two charts is a single set.

An isomorphism is a theorem, and what it carries is a law. When two constructions represent the same mathematical object, XuGo proves the isomorphism and transfers each relevant property across it using a corresponding theorem. A simple extension K(a) is isomorphic over K to K[x] modulo the minimal polynomial; localization at the powers of f is isomorphic over R to R[x] modulo f·x − 1; and interchanging the arguments of Tor gives an isomorphic object. In each case, the isomorphism is a theorem with a named map. Properties such as the vanishing of homology, exactness of a sequence or dimension are transferred by general theorems proved once from functoriality. This transfer is expressed by a theorem about a functor, not by rewriting a type, and applying it takes one line of proof. When a construction is characterized by a universal property, XuGo expresses that property as a predicate, as it does for localization and free resolutions. Any object satisfying the predicate qualifies as an instance of the construction, regardless of its encoding. The stalk of the structure sheaf at a prime is a localization of the ring at the complement of the prime because it satisfies the predicate. No isomorphism between the stalk and a ring of fractions is ever stated, because none is needed.

Little manual work remains. XuGo uses a single formulation of an isomorphism: a homomorphism that is injective and surjective. When a proof requires the inverse, it defines it by choosing preimages in the carrier and uses it only on the image, where injectivity guarantees that it is the inverse. Iterated constructions, such as sections over a basic open obtained by localizing a localization, are compared using the universal property of the first construction rather than its encoding. Some manual work remains when such comparisons must be written component by component because no bundle packages the structure. This occurs in only a few proofs, all of them short, and does not prevent an agent from proceeding to the next level of abstraction.

Objections

Higher dimensions. A reader who accepts the representation of a curve by two charts may still expect the approach to hit a wall in higher dimensions, where surfaces and varieties require arbitrary open covers, sheaves glued by a cocycle condition, and the étale topology. At the foundational level, there is no such obstacle. An integral variety of any dimension has one function field, and the coordinate ring of every affine chart is a subring of it. A torsion-free coherent sheaf of rank r is a family of lattices in the r-th power of the function field, one for each chart. A Čech cochain of any degree is a function from tuples of chart indices into the common ambient module. Passing from a curve to a surface replaces a pair of lattices with a family and a two-term complex with the Čech complex; the underlying type structure does not change. This is Weil's description of bundles by adèles, and it reflects the same set-theoretic discipline that allows the Stacks Project to dispense with universes: mathematics gave up the universe before XuGo did.

Sections over open sets are subsets of a common host type. The sections over U ∩ V and V ∩ U therefore form the same set, and the cocycle condition gij ∘ gjk = gik on a triple overlap is an equality between functions, with no need for transport. If instead the sections over an open set form a type that depends on that open set, the two intersections give rise to two types whose equality must be established by a proof. Every cocycle equation then requires a cast based on that equality. The gluing data for schemes in mathlib illustrate the cost.

A type variable serves the role of a universe. A theorem about a category on two carrier sets takes the types of its objects and morphisms as variables. Instantiating it for a presheaf category requires no increase in universe level: the Yoneda lemma holds for presheaves valued in sets of any type. When a construction must be iterated within a single type, a host type with a capacity assumption takes the place of a universe. The small étale site of a scheme consists of finitely presented algebras, which a polynomial host type like the one used for the cotangent construction can accommodate. Quasi-categories, Lurie's model of ∞-categories, are already in the corpus. What remains is labour, not a wall: capacity assumptions arise more frequently in higher dimensions, and more homological algebra is required.

Categories. Much of modern mathematics is written in the language of category theory. Yet readers familiar with that language will find no explicit category of rings in the library, no tensor–Hom adjunction, and no localization defined by representing a functor. Categorical reasoning is expressed in the form of the statements rather than through an explicit categorical object over which they quantify. The large categories of the textbook are not values in XuGo. Each construction remains on the type determined by its encoding, so the collection of rings across all types is not a set in any type. Applying a theorem to such a category would first require placing every object in a common host type, precisely the kind of transport this design avoids.

A theorem parameterized by a type variable represents a family of theorems; the type variable takes the place of quantification over the objects of a large category. The universal property of localization, ring_localization.LocalizationUniversal, allows the target ring to remain on its own type. Uniqueness up to a unique isomorphism, ring_localization.LocalizationIsomorphism, is the Yoneda argument specialized to the particular object under consideration. Functoriality, naturality and adjunctions are established once per mechanism, as illustrated in the table above. When the objects of a diagram can be represented as subsets of a common ambient space—the subfields of one closed field or the lattices in one module—inclusion is the only morphism, and every such diagram commutes. When a category is itself the object of study, it is a record like any other structure: Joyal's theorem is a statement about such a record.

The remaining cost is small. An argument that a category theorist makes once for all adjunctions is made here once per construction, with a short proof in each case. Theorems about large categories themselves, such as adjoint functor theorems, can be stated only for categories on carrier sets.

XuGo and mathlib: two library designs

This is a comparison of library designs, not of logics. mathlib has carriers too: Subgroup, Subfield and IntermediateField all represent subsets of a type. The two libraries differ in what they do next. mathlib turns the subset into a type in its own right, ↥M, equips it with structure through instances such as Field ↥M and Algebra K ↥M obtained by instance search, compares two such types using a coercion or an IsScalarTower instance, and transports across type equalities using eqToHom. XuGo stops at the carrier: the structure is a record on the set, laws are guarded by membership, and a theorem applies to a structure through a cite command. Lean permits either design, and mathlib chose the first; simple type theory permits only the second. The underlying logic dictates this choice for XuGo, and the resulting corpus is evidence that the choice suffices.

Two taxes. Lean and mathlib pay a tax of ingenuity: representing a subfield as a type, coercions between that type and its ambient field, scalar-tower instances to ensure compatibility among three fields, maps for transferring polynomials between rings, instance search to find the appropriate vector-space structure, and transport across equalities between types. XuGo pays a tax of labour: every law includes the relevant carrier-membership conditions, and the components of a structure must be written explicitly when no bundle packages them. In Galois theory, the tower law requires three IsScalarTower instances in one design and three subsets K ⊆ M ⊆ N in the other.

What remains after automation. The work here is mechanical, and the proof engine amortizes it. A bundle packages the components of a structure into a single binder, so a statement can use ∀L:field_on(A). rather than list eight operations separately. A guarded law serves as a rewrite rule, and membership of a constructed term follows from a closure rule that the engine applies automatically. The arithmetic prover works directly with rings on carrier sets, so the same procedure closes arithmetic calculations in quotient rings and in ℝ. The diagram-chasing prover performs saturation on commutative diagrams of modules on sets. mathlib also provides extensive automation: simp, lia, grind and instance search discharge most of its side conditions. The difference lies in what remains after automation. In mathlib, this involves choosing a cast lemma, an instance, a transport operation or the appropriate primed variant of an identity. In XuGo, it involves writing out comparisons component by component when no bundle packages the structure.
